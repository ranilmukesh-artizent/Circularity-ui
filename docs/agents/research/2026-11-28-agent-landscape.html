Recover and the agent landscape

28 November 2026. Internal working document. Companion to 2026-11-28-agent-failure-modes-coverage.md.

Where we are: governance of agents is our strongest area, because agents can only read, draft or hold for a person and the server refuses anything else. Integration is built but unproven, since every system behind it is a mock we wrote. Production readiness is the main gap: no production instance, no paid model access, no security or volume testing. Where the world is: market sources report that most failures of supply chain agents come from fragmented data, missing real-time integration and unverified actions, and that no single platform solves all of it. The suites are mature and wider than we are; we cover one decision, the next life of a returned asset.

Everything about the market, the failure modes and the vendors is reported by the linked sources, through a summary the product owner pasted. We have not read those pages and have not verified any of it. Numbers in brackets point to Sources. Statements about our own product are about code that exists and is tested on synthetic data; nothing has run at a customer or against a real enterprise system.

Failure mode by failure mode

The summary names three root causes behind most failures: fragmented data, no real-time integration across legacy systems, and autonomous actions nobody verified [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. These are the eight specific modes it lists.

Messy or stale master dataPartly covered

What the market reports

Sources report that messy or out-of-date master data is a main barrier to using agents in planning, and leads them to wrong answers [7, 4, 5].

What we have

  • Each source has a status (stale, unavailable, failing, sign-in expired), shown in the menu and the Fabric.
  • Conflicts between systems are recorded; an open conflict on an asset holds its decision.
  • Canonical checks run on every record. Evidence is re-checked before a decision.
  • Each figure carries where it came from (provenance, fetch log, evidence gaps).

What we do not have

  • Master data cleansing or matching across systems: no duplicate resolution, no golden record.
  • Measured behaviour on a customer's real data quality.
Siloed systems, missing contextBuilt, unproven

What the market reports

Sources report that agents fail for lack of context when systems are siloed and not integrated in real time, and that the cause is often blamed on the agent when it is integration [3, 1, 7].

What we have

  • Connection Fabric: encrypted credentials, configured connections, mappings that run, sync on triggers, webhooks.
  • An adapter registry with test and production environments, import from an OpenAPI description, MCP client sources.
  • A canonical model that every pack maps to.

What we do not have

  • A real connection to any named enterprise system: all are synthetic mocks.
  • Flat-file and EDI feeds beyond the SFTP mock.
  • Visibility beyond the systems a customer connects (no supplier tiers).
Misread business meaningPartly covered

What the market reports

A post in the summary is reported to cover how AI misreads what business terms and activity mean [8]. We only have the summary's description of it.

What we have

  • Mapping suggestions rank candidates by name and by sample values, then a model reranks them.
  • Results come in calibrated bands (pre-ticked, check, no proposal); a person confirms each one that is not certain.
  • A field can be sent to the system owner as a question.
  • Customers map and rename canonical fields; they do not add new ones.

What we do not have

  • A knowledge graph or ontology of the business.
  • Status codes and flags are mapped field by field, not learned.
Hallucination in high-stakes actionsCovered by design

What the market reports

Sources report hallucination as a risk when agents take high-stakes actions, alongside loops and ethical failures [9, 10].

What we have

  • Values, routes and rankings come from the deterministic engine, checked against 128 reference cases. The model reasons and explains; it does not produce the numbers.
  • Every agent-prepared action that is not a read or a draft is refused by the action pipeline (services/agents/forbidden.py), whatever the caller claims. A proposal holds for a person.
  • Answers say which lookups they used and whether a model wrote them.

What we do not have

  • Measured hallucination rates.
  • Evaluation sets for the model's own outputs (drafts, mapping reasons) beyond unit tests.
Multi-agent clashes and loopsAvoided by design

What the market reports

Sources report poor coordination between agents, and loops, as a failure mode that needs governance [11, 2, 6].

What we have

  • Agents of a pack run as one ordered chain of stages, not as peers negotiating.
  • Each stage has an autonomy level, limits and a confidence threshold, set in Set up and approved by a second person.
  • Run limits, budgets and cooldowns on the model router; kill switches per agent.

What we do not have

  • Agents that trade off competing goals (cost against speed against service). Nothing here needs it today, and nothing here does it.
Unclear accountability, weak guardrailsCovered

What the market reports

Sources report that it is often unclear who answers for an agent's action, and that guardrails are weak [12, 13, 1].

What we have

  • Every write goes through one action pipeline and is journalled.
  • Maker and checker are different people for setups, flows and access.
  • A changed flow runs in shadow, is replayed on past items, and starts for a share of one region with rollback.
  • Guardrails are part of a pack and apply at every autonomy level. Traces of each agent run.

What we do not have

  • An exported audit report in a form an auditor has accepted.
  • Retention rules.
Expanded attack surfacePartly covered

What the market reports

Sources report that more agents, tools and connections widen what an attacker can reach [14, 15, 12].

What we have

  • Access is enforced on the server: tenant boundary, warehouse lock, role permissions.
  • Per-agent credentials; OAuth for outside MCP clients (S256 only, tokens bound to an audience); organisation sign-in, SCIM, directory groups to roles.
  • Credentials for sources are encrypted. Model-written text is rendered without running any HTML.

What we do not have

  • Prompt-injection testing. A penetration test.
  • A review of what data leaves for the model provider (today the free tiers of two public model APIs).
  • Secrets in a managed key service.
Low planner trustCovered, untested with users

What the market reports

Sources report that planners distrust agent output and adoption stalls [2, 1, 7].

What we have

  • Each recommendation shows its evidence, cost trace and the routes it rejected.
  • Replay figures are labelled as modelled. Analytics compare predicted with actual, and count overrides.
  • Autonomy starts at "recommend only" if the customer chooses.

What we do not have

  • Evidence from real planners using it.
  • The model's explanations have not been judged by users.

The current landscape

Five platform families, as reportedopen the table

Vendor descriptions are as reported by the linked sources and are not verified by us. The market category reference is [23]. The last column is our positioning, not a feature count.

Platform Primary strength Reported handling of agent failures Trade-off Sources Against Recover
Blue Yonder Planning plus warehouse and transport execution. A supply chain knowledge graph; five domain agents under a central orchestrator; every action logged and reversible. Heavy commitment to the whole suite. [18, 19] One decision: the next life of a returned asset, and the handover to the systems of record.
Kinaxis Maestro Real-time concurrent planning; strongest in planning and disruption response. In-memory, synchronised planning data; human-in-the-loop "digital co-workers". Not deep in execution. [20] One decision: the next life of a returned asset, and the handover to the systems of record.
o9 Solutions Integrated business planning. An enterprise knowledge graph; a planning and decision layer. Relies on external ERPs and warehouse systems for execution. [19, 21] A canonical model for returns, with mappings a person confirms.
SAP and Oracle SCM Agents native to the ERP. Built-in role permissions and audit logs. Reliable only when the supply chain is standardised on that ERP. [22, 21] Independent of the ERP; connects through adapters. Less depth in any one system.
Palantir Foundry and AIP A custom ontology over messy sources. Deterministic logic tools; granular access control. Needs heavy engineering; no prebuilt supply chain apps. No link given in the summary. Templates first; a setup is five steps with an approver, not a build project.

Across all of them it is reported that most suites are multi-tenant SaaS, so strict deployment control usually means building a dedicated layer [16, 23].

Who we compete with

Platforms and suites

  • Blue Yonder, Kinaxis, o9 Solutions, SAP, Oracle, Palantir, as described above.
  • These are mature suites with a wider scope. We cover one decision: the next life of a returned asset (redeploy, resell, re-lease, parts, recycle, exit), and the handover to the systems of record. Ours is a proof of concept one week into its current build.

Closer competitors named in our own research

From docs/agents/research/2026-11-platform/C-competitive-landscape.md [26], dated 24 November 2026. Vendor claims, not tested hands-on.

  • Retail returns: Blue Yonder returns management (with Optoro), ReverseLogix, Loop, Narvar, ReturnPro, Happy Returns, Inmar. Mostly consumer and retail returns, not industrial assets.
  • Used cars: ACV, Cox Automotive, OPENLANE. That research found no product making the lessor-side lease-return decision; marked unconfirmed there.
  • Decision intelligence: Aera Technology. It has no returns content.
  • Kinaxis also appears in parked research that is out of scope [27]. Palantir is not researched in the repository.

What is missing, in the order it blocks a customer

  1. Paid model access and a production instance. Free tiers rate-limit after a few requests; a demo stalled on this.
  2. A real system connected. One live connection to a customer's ERP or warehouse system would prove the Fabric.
  3. Security testing. Prompt injection, a penetration test, and a decision on what data may go to a model provider.
  4. Volume and async. The replay has run 200 items; nothing is tested at tens of thousands. Plan 4 (async) is open.
  5. Evaluation of model outputs. Drafts and mapping reasons are tested for shape, not scored for quality.
  6. User evidence. No planner or approver outside the team has used it.

What not to claim

  • That any named enterprise system is connected. Every one is a synthetic mock.
  • "80 % done" as a measure of readiness. Most planned features exist and pass their tests; readiness for a customer is the list above.
  • Compliance with any standard. None has been assessed.

Before any of this is used with a customer, Kinective's Legal team should review it, in particular the security, audit and governance statements.
